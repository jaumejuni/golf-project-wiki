# Conclusions

After going through the entire process, there is one thing I can say with certainty: **you do not do a swap like this to save money or time**.

If your objective is simply to own a faster or more modern car, buying a good used car will almost certainly be cheaper, easier and much faster. A project like this only makes sense because you like the car and because you enjoy, or at least accept, the process involved in transforming it.

You need patience, money and, above all, time.

What you get in return, if the job is properly done, is something very different: a car that is genuinely yours, built around what you wanted rather than what a manufacturer decided to sell. The result can be something unique that you can enjoy and continue developing for many years.

## Get an engineer involved from the beginning

Probably the most important advice I can give is: **do not start a swap without first talking to the engineer who will eventually have to approve it**.

It is very easy to design the car around what is mechanically possible and only later discover that some of those decisions create problems when the car has to be homologated.

The engineer should be involved before buying the major components and certainly before making structural modifications. Knowing from the beginning what can be approved, what needs documentation and what requirements the finished car must meet can save an enormous amount of time and money later.

A technically good solution is not necessarily a solution that can be legally approved.

## Choose the right garage

The second major lesson is choosing where the work will be done.

If you have the knowledge, tools and space, doing as much of the work yourself as possible is probably the best option. You control the priorities, you know exactly what has been done and, most importantly, the car does not depend on somebody else's schedule.

If you need a professional workshop, my recommendation would be to find one specialised in **restorations, competition cars or custom car preparation**.

I would not recommend a normal general-purpose garage for a project of this scale.

Normal garages make their money from jobs that can be completed relatively quickly: servicing, brakes, clutches, repairs and similar work. A swap is exactly the opposite. The car may occupy space for months, unexpected problems appear constantly and sometimes several hours of work produce very little visible progress.

When the workshop becomes busy, a project like this naturally moves to the bottom of the queue.

That is exactly what happened with this car, and it was one of the main reasons the project took considerably longer than originally expected.

## Do not underestimate the time

My recommendation would be to plan for **two to three years from beginning to end**.

It can obviously be done faster, particularly if you already know exactly what you are doing, have completed similar conversions before and can work on the car yourself.

But that is not normally how these projects develop.

A swap of this type inevitably involves custom work. Some parts fit immediately, others almost fit, and some solutions that look perfectly reasonable on paper simply do not work once installed.

Then you modify them, test them and sometimes start again.

The same applies to the electronics, cooling, exhaust, hydraulics, brakes, ECU calibration and homologation. Solving one problem can reveal another one that was impossible to see before.

This is not necessarily a sign that the project has gone wrong. **Experimentation is part of building a car that never existed in this configuration.**

Patience is therefore not optional.

## Was it worth it?

From a purely financial point of view, probably not.

From a time perspective, definitely not.

It would have been much easier to sell the Golf and buy a newer and faster car.

But that was never really the objective.

The objective was to keep the Mk3, restore it properly and transform it into the car I wanted it to be, while retaining the character that made me want to keep it in the first place.

That is ultimately the reason to undertake a project like this.

**Don't build a swap because it is the cheapest way to get performance. Build it because you want that particular car.**

If you have the patience, the budget, the right people around you and enough time to do things properly, what you can end up with is something that cannot simply be bought: **your own version of the car, built to be enjoyed for many years to come.**
