# Conclusions

After going through the entire process, there is one thing I can say with certainty: you do not do a swap like this to save money or time. If your objective is simply to own a faster or more modern car, buying a good used car will almost certainly be cheaper, easier and much faster — a project like this only makes sense because you like the car, and because you enjoy, or at least accept, the process of transforming it. It takes patience, money and, above all, time.

What you get in return, if the job is done properly, is something very different: a car that is genuinely yours, built around what you wanted rather than what a manufacturer decided to sell — something unique that you can enjoy and keep developing for many years.

## Get an engineer involved from the beginning

Probably the most important advice I can give is: do not start a swap without first talking to the engineer who will eventually have to approve it. It is very easy to design the car around what is mechanically possible, only to discover later that some of those decisions cause problems once it comes to homologating the car.

The engineer should be involved before buying the major components, and certainly before making any structural modifications — knowing from the start what can be approved, what needs documentation, and what the finished car has to meet can save an enormous amount of time and money later on. A technically good solution is not necessarily one that can be legally approved.

## Choose the right garage

The second major lesson is choosing where the work gets done. If you have the knowledge, tools and space, doing as much of it yourself as possible is probably the best option — you control the priorities, you know exactly what has been done, and, most importantly, the car does not depend on somebody else's schedule.

If you need a professional workshop, my recommendation is to find one that specialises in restorations, competition cars or custom car preparation. I would not recommend a normal general-purpose garage for a project of this scale, because those garages make their money from jobs that can be finished relatively quickly — servicing, brakes, clutches, repairs and similar work — and a swap is exactly the opposite: the car can occupy space for months, unexpected problems keep appearing, and sometimes several hours of work produce very little visible progress. When the workshop gets busy, a project like this naturally slides to the bottom of the queue, and that is exactly what happened with this car — one of the main reasons it took considerably longer than originally expected.

## Do not underestimate the time

My recommendation is to plan for two to three years from beginning to end. It can obviously be done faster, particularly if you already know exactly what you are doing, have completed similar conversions before, and can work on the car yourself — but that is not normally how these projects go.

A swap of this type inevitably involves custom work: some parts fit immediately, others almost fit, and some solutions that look perfectly reasonable on paper simply do not work once installed. So you modify them, test them, and sometimes start again — the same goes for the electronics, cooling, exhaust, hydraulics, brakes, ECU calibration and homologation, where solving one problem can reveal another that was impossible to see before. That is not necessarily a sign the project has gone wrong; experimentation is part of building a car that never existed in this configuration before. Patience, in other words, is not optional.

## Was it worth it?

From a purely financial point of view, probably not. From a time perspective, definitely not — it would have been much easier to just sell the Golf and buy something newer and faster. But that was never really the objective. The objective was to keep the Mk3, restore it properly and turn it into the car I wanted it to be, while keeping the character that made me want to hold on to it in the first place. That, ultimately, is the reason to take on a project like this.

Do not build a swap because it is the cheapest way to get performance — build it because you want that particular car. If you have the patience, the budget, the right people around you and enough time to do things properly, what you end up with is something that cannot simply be bought: your own version of the car, built to be enjoyed for many years to come.
